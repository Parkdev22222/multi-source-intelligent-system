\section{Experiment}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Detection uses \DetectorName~\cite{sam3} with CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning}
embeddings per instance; text conditions use \LLMName~\cite{exaone4} and the
image-conditioned baseline uses \VLMName~\cite{qwen25vl}, both served through
vLLM. Change detection is evaluated on LEVIR-CD~\cite{chen2020spatial} (test,
128 tiles) and WHU-CD~\cite{ji2019fully} (test, 690 pairs); report quality on
LEVIR-CC~\cite{liu2022remote} (test, \EFourNCrops{} crops). All timings are
measured on one A100 80GB.

\textbf{The CLIP + geometry heuristic} is the rule this work replaces rather
than a published method: a fixed weighted sum of CLIP similarity, size
agreement and geodesic distance ($0.7/0.2/0.1$), with a hard radius gate and no
verifier.
The geometry-only row is the same rule with the CLIP and size terms set to
zero, leaving geodesic distance alone.

\textbf{Integrity.} Every run asserts and records four properties: train and
test pair identifiers are disjoint; their parent scenes are disjoint, so two
crops of one tile cannot straddle the split; thresholds are selected on the
validation split, never on test; and prompt style examples are drawn only from
the training split. The assertions live in the result files, not the prose.

\textbf{The external baseline is unreliable on change presence.}
In Table~\ref{tab:factuality_caption} \texttt{vlm\_direct} reaches ChgAcc
\EFourVLMChgAcc\% while producing confident, well-formed prose, at CFS-F1
\EFourVLMFOne{} against \EFourGraphRAGFOne{} for the grounded pipeline. The
error is one-sided: on the \EFourNNoChange{} crops whose references agree
nothing changed, it asserts a change anyway in \EFourVLMFalseChange\% of cases,
against \EFourGraphRAGFalseChange\%. Table~\ref{tab:example} shows a typical
one --- the failure the pipeline exists to avoid, which n-gram overlap alone
would not have surfaced.

\textbf{We lose on n-gram overlap.} Against RSICC models trained on
LEVIR-CC's own caption corpus (Table~\ref{tab:levir_cc_caption}) our BLEU-4,
ROUGE-L and CIDEr-D are well behind: those models learned the reference
phrasing, and these metrics reward phrasing. The exception is METEOR, where
each grounded condition exceeds every trained model (\texttt{vlm\_direct}, which
uses none of our pipeline, does not), because METEOR credits stems and content
words rather than exact $n$-grams. Together they suggest the pipeline names the
right objects in the wrong register --- an inference from a metric split, not a
measurement, which is why we score claims directly.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{ssec:ablation}

\textbf{Both head components are load-bearing.} Without the verifier the system
scores \EOneNoVerifierFOne{} on LEVIR-CD --- below geometry-only --- because
the head proposes matches and something has to reject them. Retrained without
cross-frame evidence it loses \EOneCrossFrameGain{} pixel F1 and
\EOneCrossFrameInstGain{} instance F1; transferred to
WHU-CD the same ablated head loses \ETwoCrossFrameGain{} and
\ETwoCrossFrameInstGain.

\textbf{Most of the headline gain is suppression, not learned matching.} The
heuristic has no verifier, so it reports every unmatched detection. A row that
pairs with the hand-tuned rule but takes state and verification from the trained
model therefore isolates the match branch (Table~\ref{tab:detection}). It reaches
\EOneHybridFOne{} on LEVIR-CD: of the
\EOneHeuristicFOne{}~$\rightarrow$~\EOnePixelFOne{} gain,
\EOneSuppressionGain{} points come from having a verifier at all and only
\EOneMatchGain{} from learning to match. WHU-CD gives \ETwoHybridFOne, a
\ETwoSuppressionGain{}/\ETwoMatchGain{} split. In instances, the unit a
counting service is judged in, WHU-CD reverses to
\ETwoSuppressionInstGain{}/\ETwoMatchInstGain{} where LEVIR-CD splits
\EOneSuppressionInstGain{}/\EOneMatchInstGain: matching earns more on the
sparser dataset.

\textbf{Aggregation is worth \EFourAggGain{} points; retrieval and graph
aggregation add little on this crop-level evaluation.} Structuring the raw
detection dump into a change inventory moves CFS-F1 from \EFourRawFOne{}
(\texttt{llm\_raw}) to \EFourStructFOne{} (\texttt{llm\_struct}). Adding
top-$k$ retrieval (\EFourFlatRagFOne) or graph aggregation
(\EFourGraphRAGFOne) moves it by less than \EFourLadderSpread{} points, and not
consistently in the graph's favor. Without paired confidence intervals we treat
this as a small observed spread and infer no ranking. The reason is structural:
all three conditions receive the same change inventory for the crop being
described, and CFS scores only claims about \emph{that} crop, so retrieved
history can move wording but rarely a claim. Of \EFourNCrops{} generations,
\EFourTextDifferPct\% differ in text between the three conditions and only
\EFourClaimsDifferPct\% differ in extracted claims. The CountMAE column of
Table~\ref{tab:factuality_caption} points the other way and we report it as
such: at crop level every aggregating condition, the graph included, carries a
larger count error than the template or the raw dump. Aggregation costs counts
here and earns them back only at neighborhood scale.

\textbf{At neighborhood level the comparison becomes decidable}
(Table~\ref{tab:e7_scene}). Generating one report per neighborhood over every
crop of a tile, the conditions stop being nested and become three
\emph{representations} of the same observations. Concatenating every crop's
inventory gives a scene count error of \ESevenStructCountMAE{} instances;
top-$k$ retrieval, which drops crops by construction, \ESevenFlatCountMAE;
graph aggregation \ESevenGraphCountMAE, better than the template's
\ESevenTemplateCountMAE{} and an order of magnitude better than either
alternative. Handed everything, the language model does not aggregate it, and
the error grows with the number of observations. On claim-level factuality
graph aggregation leads the other generated conditions (\ESevenGraphFOne{}
against \ESevenStructFOne{} and \ESevenFlatFOne) but trails the template
(\ESevenTemplateFOne): it preserves counts, rather than improving factuality
overall.
